# Fatigue, turn cadence, and the case for skipping the last run

Skiers have a proverb: *it's always the last run.*
Like most proverbs it's part truth, part survivorship — any run you
get hurt on becomes your last run by definition, which makes the
saying unfalsifiable as stated. But underneath the folk statistics
sits something worth taking seriously: **a long ski day measurably
tires your legs**, and how you ski can be tracked from your pocket.

## How does fatigue show up in ski data?

Alpy's bet is that it shows up as falling turn **cadence** — turns
per minute, per run. That's a working hypothesis, not a measured
result: a rhythm of linked turns costs energy, and straight-lining
and long lazy arcs cost less, so we expect cadence to sag as legs
tire. The app labels it a rhythm read, not a medical measure.

The tiredness itself is real, and it's tied to the kind of work
skiing is. In one
[study of 24 middle-aged recreational skiers](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5117052/),
the authors describe recreational skiing as dominated by eccentric
work — muscles lengthening under load — and measured eccentric
hamstring strength in both legs, and eccentric quadriceps strength in
one leg, still down 24 hours after about four hours of skiing.
Concentric strength didn't change significantly.

## How does Alpy measure turn cadence?

Each run's turn count is estimated from GPS heading swings
(sustained 30° course reversals — an estimate, labeled as one, and
systematically a little low for tight turns). Dividing by run
duration gives cadence, and the **Day Rhythm** chart plots it run
by run across your day.

Two honesty rules govern what the chart is allowed to claim:

- **Short runs don't count.** A 45-second connector with six
  heading wiggles would post an absurd cadence; descents under a
  minute are excluded from the rhythm data.
- **Three runs can't make a trend.** The fade verdict — "cadence
  faded 22% by day's end", comparing the first half of your runs
  with the last half — only appears with four or more comparable
  runs. With fewer, the chart shows your points and shuts up. Small samples make confident
  liars, and an instrument that cries fatigue after two runs would
  be an astrology app.

There's a known confounder the app states in-copy: terrain moves
cadence too. A steep couloir skis at a different rhythm than a
motorway groomer, so a day of varied terrain adds noise to the
trend. The verdict means most on days you lap similar terrain,
because the within-day *decline* is what it reads — and the caveat
stays printed under the chart, because deleting caveats is how
instruments become marketing.

## Adding the heart: what your watch contributes

If you ski with an Apple Watch, it
[keeps measuring your heart rate while you wear it](https://support.apple.com/guide/watch/heart-rate-apda88aefe4c/watchos),
and you can browse the day's readings in the Health app on your
iPhone — open it on the lift and read it alongside Alpy's cadence
story. (Alpy itself doesn't read Health data today; if an effort
readout ever ships, it will be built properly, not bolted on.) What
the two together mean is your call: Alpy makes no claim about it,
and leaves the interpretation to the person who was there.

## Should you skip the last run of the day?

Skip it if you're no longer skiing your morning standard. The last
run isn't cursed, and we can't give you a number for how much
riskier it is. Your own cadence data can tell you whether the fade
has started.

The honest chain of reasoning, weak links labelled: after a few
hours of skiing, eccentric leg strength is measurably down (the
study above). Its authors note, citing earlier work, that fatigue is
known to impair balance control, and they suggest — without proving
it — that this eccentric fatigue may be an injury risk factor for
recreational skiers. That doesn't prove the last run is the
dangerous one; it does make tired legs worth checking for.

The useful reframe: the question isn't "is the last run cursed?"
It's "am I skiing my 10am standard right now?" — and that's a
question your own data can actually answer. If your cadence has
faded a fifth since morning and your legs are negotiating rather
than carving, the mountain will be there tomorrow. Check the Day
Rhythm chart on the lift. If the curve has sagged, let *that* be
the last run — the one you watched happen, instead of the one that
happened to you.
