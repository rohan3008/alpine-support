# Fatigue, turn cadence, and the case for skipping the last run

Every ski culture has the same proverb: *it's always the last run.*
Like most proverbs it's part truth, part survivorship — any run you
get hurt on becomes your last run by definition, which makes the
saying unfalsifiable as stated. But underneath the folk statistics
sits a real mechanism worth taking seriously: **fatigue changes how
you ski before you notice it**, and the change is measurable from
your pocket.

## How does fatigue show up in ski data?

As falling turn **cadence** — turns per minute, per run. It drops as
the day wears on, and it drops before you'd call yourself tired,
because your brain economizes movement long before it files a
complaint. A rhythm of linked turns is a *choice* that costs energy;
straight-lining and long lazy arcs are what legs vote for when the
tank empties.

The reason is the kind of work skiing is. Skiing is eccentric
exercise — muscles lengthening under load, which is precisely the
contraction type that fatigues quadriceps fastest and shows up as
degraded control rather than felt exhaustion. (One
[study of recreational skiers](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5117052/)
measured eccentric quadriceps and hamstring strength still down 24
hours after a four-hour ski session.) You don't feel 20% weaker at
3pm; you just turn less — and that's the signature a phone can see.

## How does Alpy measure turn cadence?

Each run's turn count is estimated from GPS heading swings
(sustained 30° course reversals — an estimate, labeled as one, and
systematically a little low for tight turns). Dividing by run
duration gives cadence, and the **Day Rhythm** chart plots it run
by run across your day.

Two honesty rules govern what the chart is allowed to claim:

- **Short runs don't count.** A 45-second connector with six
  heading wiggles would post an absurd cadence; descents under a
  minute are excluded from the rhythm data.
- **Three runs can't make a trend.** The fade verdict — "cadence
  faded 22% from the first half of your day to the last" — only
  appears with four or more comparable runs. With fewer, the chart
  shows your points and shuts up. Small samples make confident
  liars, and an instrument that cries fatigue after two runs would
  be an astrology app.

There's a known confounder the app states in-copy: terrain moves
cadence too. A steep couloir skis at a different rhythm than a
motorway groomer, so a day of varied terrain adds noise to the
trend. The signal survives for most days — you mostly lap similar
terrain, and the within-day *decline* is what the verdict reads —
but the caveat stays printed under the chart, because deleting
caveats is how instruments become marketing.

## Adding the heart: what your watch contributes

If you ski with an Apple Watch, your heart rate for the day is
already sitting in the Health app — open it on the lift and read it
alongside Alpy's cadence story. (Alpy itself doesn't read Health
data today; if an effort readout ever ships, it will be built
properly, not bolted on.) The combination is where it gets
interesting:

- **Cadence falling while heart rate holds or climbs** is the
  classic fatigue picture: paying more, producing less rhythm.
- **Both falling** often just means the terrain mellowed or you're
  cruising socially. Instruments, not verdicts: the app shows the
  pattern and leaves the interpretation to the person who was there.

## Should you skip the last run of the day?

Skip it if you're no longer skiing your morning standard. The last
run isn't cursed, but the end of the day stacks real mechanisms
against you: tired legs, flat light and scraped pistes. Your own
cadence data can tell you whether the fade has started.

The honest chain of reasoning: fatigue
demonstrably degrades motor control; degraded control on skis
raises the chance of the error classes that hurt (catching an edge,
late weight shift, the knee-twisting backseat recovery); and
fatigue peaks at day's end — which is also when light flattens and
pistes are scraped to their icy base by ten thousand earlier edges.
Mechanism stacked on mechanism, all pointing the same way, no fake
precision required.

The useful reframe: the question isn't "is the last run cursed?"
It's "am I skiing my 10am standard right now?" — and that's a
question your own data can actually answer. If your cadence has
faded a fifth since morning and your legs are negotiating rather
than carving, the mountain will be there tomorrow. Check the Day
Rhythm chart on the lift. If the curve has sagged, let *that* be
the last run — the one you watched happen, instead of the one that
happened to you.
