# Fatigue, turn cadence, and the case for skipping the last run

Skiers have a proverb: *it's always the last run.* Like most proverbs
it's part truth and part survivorship. Any run you get hurt on
becomes your last run by definition, which makes the saying
unfalsifiable as stated. Underneath the folk statistics is something
worth taking seriously, though: a long ski day measurably tires your
legs, and you can track how you ski from your pocket.

## How does fatigue show up in ski data?

Alpy's bet is that it shows up as falling turn cadence, meaning turns
per minute, per run. That's a working hypothesis and we haven't
measured it. A rhythm of linked turns costs energy, while
straight-lining and long lazy arcs cost less, so we expect cadence to
sag as legs tire. The app labels it a rhythm read, not a medical
measure.

The tiredness itself is real, and it comes from the kind of work
skiing is. In one
[study of 24 middle-aged recreational skiers](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5117052/),
the authors describe recreational skiing as dominated by eccentric
work (muscles lengthening under load). They measured eccentric
hamstring strength in both legs, and eccentric quadriceps strength in
one leg, still down 24 hours after about four hours of skiing.
Concentric strength didn't change significantly.

## How does Alpy measure turn cadence?

Each run's turn count is estimated from GPS heading swings, meaning
sustained 30° course reversals. It's an estimate, labeled as one, and
systematically a little low for tight turns. Dividing by run duration
gives cadence, and the Day Rhythm chart plots it run by run across
your day.

Two honesty rules limit what the chart is allowed to claim. First,
short runs don't count. A 45-second connector with six heading
wiggles would post an absurd cadence, so descents under a minute are
excluded from the rhythm data.

Second, three runs can't make a trend. The fade verdict ("cadence
faded 22% by day's end", comparing the first half of your runs with
the last half) only appears with four or more comparable runs. With
fewer, the chart shows your points and says nothing else. Small
samples make confident liars, and an instrument that cried fatigue
after two runs would be an astrology app.

The app also states a known confounder in its copy: terrain moves
cadence too. A steep couloir skis at a different rhythm than a
motorway groomer, so a day of varied terrain adds noise to the trend.
The verdict means most on days you lap similar terrain, because what
it reads is the *decline* within the day. That caveat stays printed
under the chart, because deleting caveats is how instruments become
marketing.

## Adding the heart: what your watch contributes

If you ski with an Apple Watch, it
[keeps measuring your heart rate while you wear it](https://support.apple.com/guide/watch/heart-rate-apda88aefe4c/watchos),
and you can browse the day's readings in the Health app on your
iPhone. Open it on the lift and read it alongside Alpy's cadence
chart. (Alpy itself doesn't read Health data today. If an effort
readout ever ships, it will be built properly, not bolted on.) Alpy
makes no claim about what the two mean together; you were there, so
the interpretation is yours.

## Should you skip the last run of the day?

Skip it if you're no longer skiing your morning standard. The last
run isn't cursed, and we can't give you a number for how much riskier
it is. Your own cadence data can tell you whether the fade has
started.

Here's the reasoning, with the weak links labelled. After a few hours
of skiing, eccentric leg strength is measurably down (the study
above). Its authors note, citing earlier work, that fatigue is known
to impair balance control, and they suggest, without proving it, that
this eccentric fatigue may be an injury risk factor for recreational
skiers. That doesn't prove the last run is the dangerous one. It does
make tired legs worth checking for.

So instead of asking whether the last run is cursed, ask whether
you're skiing your 10am standard right now. Your own data can answer
that one. If your cadence has faded a fifth since morning and your
legs are negotiating the turns more than carving them, the mountain
will be there tomorrow. Check the Day Rhythm chart on the lift. If the
curve has sagged, let *that* be the last run: the one you watched
happen, instead of the one that happened to you.
