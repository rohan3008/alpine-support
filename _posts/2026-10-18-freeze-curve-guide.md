# The freeze curve: the one chart that explains your ski day

Ask an experienced skier what makes a great day and they'll talk
about snow quality before snow quantity. Twenty fresh centimetres
that fell *wet* ski like mashed potato; five that fell cold ski like
silk. The variable behind almost all of it is one number: the
**freezing level** — the altitude where the air crosses 0 °C — and
the story of a ski day is largely the story of where that line sits
and how it moves.

Alpy draws this as the *freeze curve*: freezing-level altitude,
hour by hour, across three days, with your resort's base and summit
marked and a rule at "now." Here's how to read it.

## Reading the three positions

**Freeze line below the base.** The whole mountain is in
sub-freezing air. New snow falls dry everywhere; existing snow
stays wintry. On a storm day this is the jackpot reading — flakes
at the valley floor.

**Freeze line between base and summit** — the interesting and most
common case. The mountain is split: above the line, winter; below
it, spring. Rain at the bottom can be snow up top (the saddest lift
queue in skiing is the one standing in drizzle below a snowline you
can *see*). This reading decides where to ski: upper-mountain laps
hold quality while lower runs go heavy.

**Freeze line above the summit.** The whole hill is thawing.
Surfaces soften progressively through the day — which is not
automatically bad news, and that's where timing comes in.

## The daily cycle: corn and boilerplate

In settled spring weather the freeze curve breathes daily: up
through the afternoon, down overnight. The snowpack follows with a
lag, melting by day and refreezing by night, and that cycle
manufactures both the best and worst pistes of the season.

The sequence on a clear spring day: dawn surfaces are *boilerplate*
— yesterday's melt frozen into chattery armor. As the sun and the
rising freeze line work on it, the top centimetre loosens into
*corn*: hero snow, edge-friendly and forgiving. Stay past midday
and corn collapses into slush that grabs your skis and ruins your
knees' afternoon.

The freeze curve is how you time it. Overnight dip well below the
summit → a proper refreeze → expect boilerplate early, corn
mid-morning, slush after lunch; aspect shifts the schedule (east
faces ripen first, west last). No overnight dip at all → no
refreeze → the snowpack never reset, and the day starts where
yesterday ended: heavy. One glance at whether last night's curve
dipped under your resort's base tells you which movie you're in.

## Storms: the rain–snow line is a freeze line

During a storm, the freeze curve *is* the rain–snow line, give or
take a couple hundred metres (falling snow survives a little below
the 0 °C altitude before melting). A storm that arrives warm and
cools — curve stepping down through the hours — lays wet snow first
and dry snow on top: right-side-up powder, the good kind. A storm
that warms as it passes builds the inverse: dense over light,
upside-down snow that skis strangely and, on steep terrain, is
exactly the loading pattern avalanche bulletins get animated about.
The curve's *direction during the storm hours* tells you which
you're getting tomorrow.

## Where the numbers come from, honestly

Alpy's curve is built from [Open-Meteo](https://open-meteo.com)
forecast data — model output, refreshed through the day, for your
resort's exact coordinates. Model freezing levels are good and not
gospel: local effects (cold air pooling in valleys, föhn winds) can
shift reality a hundred metres or more from the model, and the app
does not pretend otherwise — the curve is labeled with its source,
and it's a planning instrument, not a promise. The summit
temperature shown beside it is the same model sampled at your
summit's elevation, which is why it routinely reads 8–10 °C colder
than the village forecast your phone's weather app shows. (Standard
lapse rate is roughly 6.5 °C per 1,000 m — over Zermatt's 2,263 m
of lift-served vertical, that's a different climate, not a
different forecast.)

## Using it in practice

The night before: check whether the curve dips below base
overnight (refreeze?) and where it sits at 09:00 (which half of the
mountain gets winter?). Storm inbound: watch the curve's trend
through the storm hours (right-side-up or upside-down?). Spring
day: plan runs *down* the mountain as the day warms, following the
corn window from high east faces to low west ones, and be on the
deck before the slush wins.

One line on a chart, and most of a ski day's decisions fall out of
it. That's the best kind of instrument.
