# The 2026/27 season-opening checklist

First chair of the season is coming. This checklist covers your
body, your gear, the paperwork, safety kit and your phone, and it
gives the reasoning for each item, because you're more likely to
actually run a checklist you understand.

## How to get ready for ski season, step by step

Start about six weeks before first chair: leg work and a binding
check first, then pass math, insurance and avalanche-kit practice two
weeks out, then charge, cache and set alerts the night before. The
timings are our suggestion; the one hard deadline is pass pricing,
which can step up through autumn (details below).

1. Six weeks out, start the leg work. Slow eccentric squats,
   step-downs, wall-sits and balance work, two or three short
   sessions a week.
2. Six weeks out, book the binding check. Release tested against
   your DIN, edges, bases and wax while you're there; try your boots
   on.
3. Two weeks out, do the pass math and sort insurance. Realistic
   days times honest day-ticket prices, and cover that explicitly
   includes piste rescue.
4. Two weeks out, rebuild the bulletin habit and run a transceiver
   drill. Fresh batteries, one genuine practice search.
5. The night before, charge, cache and set thresholds. Phone at
   100%, trail map cached, powder alert set.
6. On the first morning, track the baseline day, and ski like it's
   the first day, because the data will cheerfully confirm that it
   is.

## What training should you do before ski season?

Train the eccentric contraction skiing actually uses, where muscles
lengthen under load. A
[study of recreational skiers](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5117052/)
notes that eccentric knee-extensor (quadriceps) activity is dominant
during turns, and its 24 middle-aged intermediate skiers still had
reduced eccentric hamstring strength (and, in one leg, quadriceps
strength) 24 hours after a four-hour session. The authors suggest
eccentric training "may be warranted" for recreational skiers.

Our suggested routine is simple: slow downhill-phase squats and
step-downs (take three to four seconds on the way down), reverse
lunges, wall-sits, and single-leg balance work, with eyes closed once
it gets easy. Two or three short sessions a week, with rest days
between.

## Do ski bindings need checking every season?

We'd say yes. It's the one item we treat as non-negotiable: have a
shop check your bindings every season, so the release setting is
confirmed before you need it.

The release setting (the "DIN" number) is chosen from your weight,
height, age, skier type and boot sole length, the parameters listed
in the free preview of the
[ISO 11088 shop standard](https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/71932/a1cc6d2778bc4e65afc9610f902a78d5/ISO-11088-2018.pdf),
so tell the shop if any of those changed. While you're there, get
the edges deburred, the bases ground if last spring's rocks left
their signature, and a fresh wax.

Boots: check liners for packed-out heel pockets and buckle teeth for
wear. Try boots on an evening *before* the trip, not in a rental
queue at altitude.

Helmet: Smith says
[most manufacturers recommend replacing within 3–5 years](https://smithopticsaustralia.com/blogs/news/when-to-replace-your-ski-helmet-a-guide-from-smith),
and immediately after a significant fall or collision, because the
internal foam can be compressed even when the shell looks intact.
If you can't remember buying it, replace it.

## Paperwork: the pass math has a deadline

Some multi-resort passes price-step upward through autumn (as we
write,
[epicpass.com](https://www.epicpass.com/) carries a "Prices Go Up
November 15" banner), so the arithmetic is worth twenty minutes now
rather than in December. Count your realistic days, multiply by
honest day-ticket prices at your target resorts, and compare. One
2026/27 wrinkle: on our [resort pages](/resorts/), checked against
the official pass sites in October 2026, big Alpine names on Ikon
such as Zermatt, Chamonix and Kitzbühel are *limited-days* (7 days
on the Ikon Pass, 5 on the Base Pass, per
[Ikon's destinations page](https://www.ikonpass.com/en/destinations)).
That's fine for a trip but won't cover a season. And if you ski in
Europe, get carrier or travel insurance that explicitly covers piste
rescue and repatriation.

## Safety: the bulletin habit and the beeping drill

There are two pre-season items for anyone who leaves the groomers.
First, get back into the habit of reading the bulletin before the
season builds its snowpack. Find your region's official avalanche
bulletin ([SLF](https://www.slf.ch/en/) for Switzerland,
[avalanche.org](https://avalanche.org/) for the US) and start reading it weekly
from the first storms. Persistent weak layers
[can last weeks to months](https://www.avalanches.org/standards/avalanche-problems/),
and it's better to follow one from the start than to meet it by
surprise. Our [bulletin-reading primer](/blog/reading-avalanche-bulletins/)
covers the five-step scale, aspects and elevations.

Second, if you carry rescue kit, put fresh batteries in the
transceiver and do one genuine practice search (garden, park,
anywhere), so your first search of the season isn't a real one.

## Phone: five minutes before first chair

The sensors in your pocket need a little setup before day one:

- Apple designs iPhone for 0–35 °C ambient and says very cold
  conditions outside that range
  [can temporarily shorten battery life](https://support.apple.com/en-us/118431).
  Start at 100% and carry it in an inside pocket.
- A tracking app will ask for location and motion access on first
  start. Grant them at breakfast, not with gloves on in a gondola.
- Download or cache your resort's trail map before leaving wifi. In
  Alpy, catalog resorts' piste maps and local guides ship inside the
  app; a piste map fetched live is kept on the phone for 30 days, and
  the last conditions it fetched show (with their timestamp) when
  there are no bars.
- Set your fresh-snow powder alert threshold before the season's
  first storm, not after reading about it.
- Track your first day back even though you'll ski it slowly. It's
  the season's baseline. Every PR and fatigue trend for the rest of
  the winter needs something to be measured against, and day one is
  where the record book opens.
