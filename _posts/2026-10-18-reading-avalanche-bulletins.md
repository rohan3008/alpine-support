# How to read an avalanche bulletin

**This article is not avalanche training.** If you ski off-piste,
take an avalanche course and carry (and practice with) transceiver,
shovel and probe. What follows is only how to read the public
bulletin — the single most ignored free document in skiing.

## The parts that matter

**The danger level (1–5).** Low, Moderate, Considerable, High,
Extreme — a European and North American standard. The non-obvious
part: the scale isn't linear. Most avalanche fatalities happen at
level 3, *Considerable*, partly because level 3 days look inviting
and partly because people read "3 of 5" as "fine."

**Critical aspects.** Bulletins name the compass directions where the
problem lives — "NW through NE above 2,400 m" is a different mountain
than "all aspects." Aspect is the direction a slope *faces*.

**Elevation bands.** The same storm builds different snowpacks at
1,800 m and 2,800 m. The bulletin tells you where the danger starts.

**The problem type.** Wind slab, persistent weak layer, wet snow —
each implies different terrain choices and different timing.

## Why apps should show the original

A summary of a bulletin is a liability pretending to be a service.
Alpy shows the official bulletin for your resort's region — from
[SLF](https://www.slf.ch) in Switzerland and
[avalanche.org](https://avalanche.org) in the US — with the full
five-step scale, the stated aspects and elevations, and the issuing
service named. Where no official bulletin covers a region, the app
says exactly that instead of inventing reassurance. An honest "no
bulletin here" is information too: it means *you* are the forecaster
today, and you should probably ski the pistes.
