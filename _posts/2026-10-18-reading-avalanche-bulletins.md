# How to read an avalanche bulletin

**This article is not avalanche training.** If you ski off-piste,
take an avalanche course and carry (and practice with) transceiver,
shovel and probe. What follows is only how to read the public
bulletin — the single most ignored free document in skiing.

## How to read an avalanche bulletin, step by step

Read it in four passes: the danger level, the compass aspects where
the problem lives, the elevations where it starts, then the problem
type.
Together they say how dangerous today is, where on the mountain, and
what kind of avalanche to expect — which is what turns a number into
terrain choices.

1. **Read the danger level (1–5).** Low, Moderate, Considerable,
   High, then Very high in Europe or Extreme in North America — the
   same five-level scale on both continents (see the
   [European scale](https://www.avalanches.org/standards/avalanche-danger-scale/)
   and the
   [North American scale](https://avalanche.org/avalanche-encyclopedia/human/resources/north-american-public-avalanche-danger-scale/)).
   The non-obvious part: the scale isn't linear. Most avalanche
   fatalities happen at level 3, *Considerable* — around half of
   them, by [SLF's count](https://www.slf.ch/en/avalanche-bulletin-and-snow-situation/about-the-avalanche-bulletin/danger-levels/) —
   partly because level 3 days look inviting and partly because
   people read "3 of 5" as "fine."
2. **Find the critical aspects.** Bulletins name the compass
   directions where the problem lives — "NW through NE above
   2,400 m" is a different mountain than "all aspects." Aspect is the
   direction a slope *faces*.
3. **Check the elevation bands.** The same storm builds different
   snowpacks at 1,800 m and 2,800 m. The bulletin tells you where the
   danger starts.
4. **Read the problem type.** Wind slab, persistent weak layer, wet
   snow — each implies different terrain choices and different
   timing. The
   [EAWS avalanche problems](https://www.avalanches.org/standards/avalanche-problems/)
   page gives the standard definitions and the typical advice for
   each.

## Should a ski app summarize the avalanche bulletin?

No. A summary of a bulletin is a liability pretending to be a
service. Alpy shows the official bulletin for your resort's region —
from [SLF](https://www.slf.ch) in Switzerland and
[avalanche.org](https://avalanche.org) in the US — with the full
five-step scale, the stated aspects and elevations, and the issuing
service named.

Where no official bulletin covers a region, the app
says exactly that instead of inventing reassurance. An honest "no
bulletin here" is information too: it means *you* are the forecaster
today, and you should probably ski the pistes.
